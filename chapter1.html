Application data → transport segment → IP datagram → link frame → signals; the receiver removes the headers in reverse.

KEY DISTINCTION

Keep transmission delay (bits/rate), propagation delay (distance/speed), queueing, and processing delay conceptually separate.

1.1 The Internet is an edge connected by a core

End systems run applications at the network edge. Access networks connect those hosts to a first router. The network core is a mesh of packet switches that moves packets hop by hop.

DESIGN INSIGHT

KEEP THE CAUSES SEPARATE

Transmission delay depends on packet size and link rate. Propagation delay depends on distance and signal speed.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A 1,500-byte packet contains 12,000 bits. On a 10 Mb/s link, transmission delay is 12,000 / 10,000,000 = 1.2 ms. Across 2,000 km of fiber at 2 × 10⁸ m/s, propagation delay is 2,000,000 / 200,000,000 = 10 ms. Add these to processing and queueing delay for total nodal delay.

1.6 The Internet evolved in an adversarial world

Connected systems face malware, vulnerable services, credential theft, packet sniffing, spoofing, man-in-the-middle attacks, and denial of service. Security is therefore an architectural concern rather than a feature added at the end.

How the design grew

Packet switching research in the 1960s led to early shared networks. Internetworking and TCP/IP connected unlike networks in the 1970s and 1980s. The Web drove explosive public growth in the 1990s. Broadband, cloud data centers, content networks, mobile access, IoT, and software-defined infrastructure now shape the Internet’s flatter, service-centered topology.

ENDURING PRINCIPLE

The Internet succeeds by joining independently operated networks through common protocols. Its architecture must tolerate heterogeneous links, decentralized administration, failures, and continuous evolution.

Concept clinic Applied questions: Internet foundations

Each answer begins with the governing principle, then develops the mechanism and its practical consequence.

01What is the difference between baseband and broadband transmission?

Baseband uses the available channel as one digital communication channel. Broadband divides a wider frequency range into separate bands and usually modulates data onto carrier frequencies.

In baseband transmission, digital pulses are placed directly on the medium. Only one signal normally occupies the channel at a time, although users may still share it in time. Traditional Ethernet over twisted pair is a familiar baseband system. Repeaters or switches extend the network, and the signaling method determines how bits become voltage or light changes.

In broadband transmission, frequency-division multiplexing allows several channels to exist simultaneously. Cable Internet can carry television channels and downstream/upstream data in different frequency bands. Radio, cellular, and Wi-Fi also allocate parts of the spectrum to channels and modulate carriers.

Baseband asks: “Who uses this channel now?” Broadband asks: “Which frequency band does each signal occupy?” The words describe signaling and spectrum use, not whether the service itself is fast.

02What data rate does each guided and unguided communication medium provide?

A medium has no single fixed data rate. Achievable rate depends on bandwidth, signal-to-noise ratio, distance, coding, modulation, equipment, channel width, and the protocol generation.

  • Twisted pair: telephone access may deliver tens to hundreds of Mb/s; Ethernet commonly provides 100 Mb/s, 1 Gb/s, 2.5/5 Gb/s, and 10 Gb/s over suitable categories and distances.
  • Coaxial cable: shared cable-access systems can provide hundreds of Mb/s to multi-Gb/s service; the neighborhood capacity is shared.
  • Optical fiber: access links commonly provide 1–10 Gb/s, while backbone links carry 100, 400, or more Gb/s per wavelength and combine multiple wavelengths.
  • Wi-Fi: real throughput ranges from tens of Mb/s to over 1 Gb/s depending on generation, channel width, signal quality, contention, and client capability.
  • Cellular: practical rates range from a few Mb/s under weak or loaded coverage to hundreds of Mb/s or more under favorable 4G/5G conditions.
  • Microwave and satellite: rates range from Mb/s to multi-Gb/s. Distance, spectrum allocation, weather, antenna geometry, and shared capacity matter.

Published link rates are physical-layer signaling rates. Application throughput is lower because headers, retransmissions, contention, congestion, and server limits consume capacity.

03How are contention and congestion different? Can high contention cause congestion?

Contention is competition for a shared resource. Congestion occurs when offered traffic exceeds what a network resource can serve, causing queues, delay, and possibly loss.

Wi-Fi stations contend for the same radio channel: each waits, senses the channel, and may back off. Even before a router queue is congested, this competition can reduce each station’s share and increase access delay. Congestion usually refers to overloaded queues or paths inside the packet network, such as an output link receiving packets faster than it transmits them.

High contention can contribute to congestion because repeated attempts, bursts, or many active senders increase offered load. It does not always create congestion: a well-scheduled or lightly loaded shared medium can have contention without persistent queue growth. Congestion can also happen on a dedicated link where many upstream flows converge, even if the final link has no contention protocol.

04Which data structures store routing and forwarding information?

Routers usually keep a control-plane Routing Information Base (RIB) and a fast data-plane Forwarding Information Base (FIB).

The RIB contains candidate routes learned from connected networks, static configuration, and routing protocols. It stores attributes used to compare routes. After route selection, the router programs the FIB with the best forwarding choices. An IPv4 or IPv6 lookup uses longest-prefix matching, so software implementations often use prefix trees or compressed tries. High-speed hardware commonly uses TCAM or specialized lookup memory for parallel matching.

The FIB normally points to a next-hop or adjacency structure containing the outgoing interface and link-layer rewrite information. ARP for IPv4 or Neighbor Discovery for IPv6 maintains the mapping from a next-hop IP address to a local link-layer address. These tables cooperate but serve different purposes.

05How does a router’s forwarding table get populated?

The control plane learns possible routes, selects the best route for each prefix, and installs the result into the FIB used by the forwarding plane.

  1. Connected routes appear when an interface is configured and operational.
  2. Static routes are entered by an administrator or controller.
  3. Dynamic protocols such as OSPF or IS-IS discover paths inside an organization; BGP exchanges reachability between autonomous systems.
  4. Selection compares protocol preference, prefix length, metric, policy, and reachability of the next hop.
  5. Programming converts the selected route into hardware-friendly FIB and adjacency entries.

When topology or policy changes, the control plane reconverges and replaces affected entries. Packets do not run a routing algorithm themselves; they use the already computed FIB at each hop.

06How do circuit switching and packet switching differ?

Circuit switching reserves a predictable share of resources for a session. Packet switching shares links statistically, sending packets when capacity is available.

A circuit is established before user data, and reserved time slots or frequency bands remain assigned for the session. This gives predictable capacity and delay after setup, but idle periods waste the reservation. Packet switching divides data into packets. Routers store, queue, and forward each packet; many bursty users efficiently share the same links, but queueing delay and loss can vary.

Modern IP networks use packet switching. Applications that need steady performance obtain it through sufficient capacity, traffic engineering, scheduling, admission control, or adaptive behavior rather than through a classic end-to-end circuit.

Ten users who each transmit only 10% of the time fit efficiently on a shared packet link. Ten dedicated circuits would reserve capacity even during the other 90%.

07Does point-to-point communication pass through routers?

“Point-to-point” describes one link or logical relationship with exactly two endpoints. It does not mean an entire Internet path contains no routers.

A serial fiber between two routers is a point-to-point link. A home host communicating with a web server has end-to-end communication between two application endpoints, but its packets commonly cross many routers. The path can therefore be a chain of point-to-point and shared links.

If two devices are on the same IP subnet and local link, they can exchange frames directly after ARP or Neighbor Discovery, so no router forwards that traffic. If the destination lies outside the local subnet, the host sends the frame to its default gateway, and routers make hop-by-hop forwarding decisions until the packet reaches the destination network.

ENGINEERING PERSPECTIVE

Architectural ideas that explain real networks

These ideas connect the formulas and protocol names to the decisions engineers make when networks grow, fail, or cross administrative boundaries.

ARCHITECTURE

The end-to-end principle is a placement rule

Functions that require application knowledge—such as confirming that a file is complete—must ultimately be checked at the endpoints. A link checksum can prove that one frame crossed one link, but it cannot prove that the correct file reached the correct process.

This does not mean the network should contain no features. Routers still forward, queue, filter, measure, and sometimes translate traffic. The principle asks whether putting a function inside the network can provide a complete guarantee. If endpoints must check again anyway, the in-network version is an optimization rather than the final authority.

DESIGN TESTIf only the application can know whether the goal was achieved, preserve an endpoint check even when lower layers help.
TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN
  • Scope of a guarantee: an Ethernet CRC covers one frame on one hop; TCP checksum and sequence state cover a transport byte stream; a content hash can cover the final object. Each wider scope detects faults that the narrower scope cannot observe.
  • Placement tradeoff: hop-by-hop recovery is useful on a lossy radio link because it repairs quickly, but end-to-end reliability is still required because packets may be lost after that hop.
  • Protocol consequence: encryption, duplicate suppression, transaction identity, and application validation should survive route changes and middlebox replacement. Network assistance may accelerate them but must not become the only correctness check.
complete correctness = lower-layer assistance + final endpoint verification
STATE

Fate sharing and soft state improve recovery

Connection state stored only in a router can disappear when that router fails. The fate-sharing idea keeps essential communication state with the endpoints whose communication depends on it. The network can then reroute packets without reconstructing every application relationship in the core.

Many control-plane records are soft state: they expire unless refreshed. Expiration turns silent failure into automatic cleanup, but refresh traffic and timer choices become part of correctness. Long timers preserve stale information; short timers increase churn and may mistake delay for failure.

TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN

A router’s forwarding entries, ARP/ND cache, NAT bindings, and firewall connection records have different lifetimes and failure consequences. Stateless IP forwarding can reroute immediately after control-plane convergence; a stateful middlebox may break the flow because the replacement has no matching binding.

  • Soft-state refresh: routing adjacencies use hello/dead timers; ARP/ND entries age; NAT mappings expire after inactivity.
  • Convergence sequence: detect failure → originate an update → flood or advertise it → recompute best paths → program the FIB.
  • Operational tension: aggressive timers detect failures faster but consume resources and increase false positives during transient congestion.
PERFORMANCE

Average utilization hides tail latency

A link can look healthy on average and still produce severe delay during short bursts. Queueing is nonlinear: as offered load approaches service capacity, small arrival fluctuations create disproportionately long waits. This is why a network at 60% average use can still have poor 99th-percentile latency.

Capacity planning therefore considers burst size, traffic correlation, queue discipline, and high-percentile delay—not only mean throughput. Statistical multiplexing is efficient because users are not always active, but synchronized bursts reduce that advantage.

TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN

For the idealized M/M/1 queue, mean time in the system grows as service utilization approaches one. Real traffic is often burstier than the model, so the formula is a warning rather than a prediction.

mean system time ≈ 1 / (μ − λ), where λ is arrival rate and μ is service rate
  • Microbursts can overflow a shallow switch buffer even when one-minute utilization is low.
  • Correlated traffic—many clients reacting to the same event—breaks the assumption that independent users smooth one another.
  • Report p50, p95, p99, and p99.9 latency with queue drops and ECN marks; an average alone can hide the user-visible failures.
CONTROL

Forwarding is mechanism; routing is policy plus computation

The data plane performs a local operation: match a packet and apply an action. The control plane decides which actions should exist. A shortest path is only one input. Operators also consider business relationships, security, traffic-engineering goals, maintenance, and failure containment.

Across the Internet, the selected BGP route is often the most acceptable policy path rather than the mathematically shortest path. Reachability is therefore both a graph problem and an agreement among independently operated networks.

TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN

A FIB entry usually contains a destination prefix, next-hop group, output interface, and rewrite/adjacency reference. Longest-prefix match selects the most specific prefix. Equal-cost next hops are often selected by hashing the flow five-tuple so packets within one flow remain ordered.

  • Inside an AS: OSPF or IS-IS computes topology-based paths from link-state data.
  • Between ASes: BGP propagates prefixes with attributes such as AS_PATH, LOCAL_PREF, MED, communities, and next hop.
  • Policy: import filters decide which routes are accepted; best-path policy decides which route is used; export policy decides which reachability is announced to each neighbor.
PACKET SIZE

MTU is a hidden end-to-end constraint

Every link limits the largest frame it can carry. IP packets larger than a path component supports may require fragmentation, a smaller packet, or an error that enables Path MTU Discovery. If the necessary control message is filtered, a connection can establish successfully yet stall on larger transfers—a classic PMTU black hole.

Tunnels add headers and reduce effective MTU. Modern transports often size packets conservatively and probe upward. Packet size trades header efficiency against serialization delay, loss cost, and compatibility.

TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN

IPv4 routers may fragment packets unless the Don't Fragment bit forbids it. IPv6 routers do not fragment transit packets; the source must send a suitable size after receiving an ICMPv6 Packet Too Big message. Transport MSS is payload-oriented and normally derived from interface MTU minus IP and TCP headers.

  • Ethernet’s common MTU is 1500 bytes; encapsulation by VLANs, VPNs, GRE, or other tunnels consumes additional bytes.
  • TCP advertises MSS during the SYN exchange. QUIC endpoints send UDP datagrams and perform Datagram Packetization Layer PMTU Discovery.
  • A PMTU black hole often appears as “small requests work, large responses hang” because oversized packets disappear while the connection itself remains established.
OBSERVABILITY

A path is not one stable, symmetric object

Packets in the two directions can follow different routes because each network applies its own policy. Equal-cost multipath may also hash different flows onto different links. Traceroute reports responses from control messages that routers may rate-limit or return along another path.

Consequently, one measurement is evidence about a particular flow at a particular time. It is not a permanent map. Sound diagnosis correlates endpoint timing, interface counters, routing state, packet capture, and repeated measurements.

TECHNICAL DRILL-DOWN

Classic traceroute varies the IP TTL. Router n decrements TTL to zero and returns ICMP Time Exceeded; the destination returns a terminal response. The displayed RTT covers the probe’s forward path plus the ICMP reply path, which may differ.

  • Per-flow ECMP hashes addresses, ports, and protocol; changing probe ports can reveal different next hops.
  • Control-plane policing can delay or suppress ICMP without affecting forwarded user traffic.
  • Route changes during a trace can create apparent loops or missing hops. MPLS, tunnels, and hidden layer-2 segments can obscure intermediate topology.

Use Paris traceroute for stable flow identifiers, packet capture for endpoint truth, and routing telemetry to explain which control-plane event changed the path.

Advanced analysis Architecture, performance, and operational implications

This section connects standards, ISP structure, statistical performance, and resource sharing to the behavior of operational networks.

Standards turn independent implementations into one Internet
IETF · RFC · INTEROPERABILITY

A protocol needs more than a name: independently built endpoints must agree on field encoding, state transitions, timers, error behavior, and extensibility. The Internet Engineering Task Force develops many Internet standards through open working groups. Their documents are published as RFCs; an RFC can be a standard, an experimental design, an operational recommendation, or an informational record.

Interoperability testing matters because two implementations may interpret an edge case differently even when both follow the main message format. Robust protocol design specifies how unknown fields are ignored or negotiated, how versions change, and which behavior is mandatory.

Concept: a protocol specification defines syntax, semantics, ordering, state, timeout behavior, and evolution rules.

Why the Internet is hierarchical even though IP is universal
ACCESS · TRANSIT · PEERING

A full mesh among access networks would require a quadratic number of relationships. Instead, access ISPs buy transit from larger providers, networks peer at Internet exchange points, and content providers connect directly or place caches inside access networks.

  • Customer–provider: the customer pays for reachability to the wider Internet.
  • Peering: networks exchange traffic directly according to commercial and technical policy.
  • Private backbone: a large service provider carries traffic over its own long-haul network and exits near the user.

BGP expresses this policy-rich connectivity. The result is scalable, but the path chosen by policy may differ from the shortest geographic path.

Queueing must be measured as a distribution
TRAFFIC INTENSITY · BURSTS · TAILS

The ratio La/R compares offered load with link capacity, where L is average packet length, a is average packet arrival rate, and R is transmission rate. When the long-run ratio exceeds one, no finite queue can remain stable. As it approaches one, delay becomes highly sensitive to burst timing.

Mean delay alone is incomplete. Engineers track variance and the probability that delay exceeds a service objective. Packet loss probability also rises as buffers fill. Retransmission can recover data, but it adds load and can worsen congestion if the sender does not reduce its rate.

Measure together: throughput, mean delay, tail delay, jitter, queue occupancy, drop rate, and retransmitted bytes.

FDM and TDM are two ways to construct a circuit
RESOURCE RESERVATION

Frequency-division multiplexing assigns each circuit a frequency band for the session. Time-division multiplexing divides time into repeating frames and assigns each circuit one or more slots. In either case, the circuit obtains a predictable rate even when it has no data to send.

Packet switching instead schedules packets on demand. Statistical multiplexing improves utilization for bursty sources but produces queueing. A modern packet network can approximate circuit-like treatment with admission control, shaping, priority queues, or reserved capacity, yet ordinary TCP connection setup by itself reserves no network resources.

Clouds and content networks changed the shape of the Internet
PRIVATE NETWORKS · EDGE DELIVERY

Large service providers operate data centers, private wide-area backbones, edge points of presence, and direct peering links. Traffic may enter the provider’s network close to the user and remain there until it reaches a selected data center. This reduces dependence on third-party transit and gives the provider control over routing, capacity, replication, and failover.

Cloud platforms combine computing and storage with high-performance internal networks. The application’s observed latency can therefore depend more on region choice, edge placement, and private backbone routing than on the public Internet core alone.

Technical reference Internet architecture and performance

Use this section as the compact reference for the chapter. It connects terminology, calculations, protocol behavior, and the decisions made at each layer.

INTERNET MODEL

Infrastructure and service

The Internet is a network of networks. End systems run applications at the edge; packet switches forward traffic through the core; access networks connect homes, campuses, enterprises, and mobile devices to an ISP. From an application’s view, the Internet offers a communication service through sockets. A protocol defines message format, message order, actions taken on receipt, and behavior on timeout or error.

  • Host: endpoint that runs network applications.
  • Router: forwards packets between networks.
  • Link: communication channel with a transmission rate.
  • ISP: an administrative network connected to other ISPs.
EDGE AND ACCESS

How a device reaches the first router

Residential access commonly uses cable, fiber, fixed wireless, or cellular. Institutions use switched Ethernet and Wi-Fi. A shared access medium divides capacity among active users, while a dedicated point-to-point link reserves its physical channel. Guided media include twisted pair, coaxial cable, and fiber; unguided media include terrestrial radio, microwave, and satellite. Rate, distance, interference, deployment cost, and mobility determine the choice.

A router first receives packet bits, processes the header, waits for an outgoing link, then transmits. Propagation describes bit travel through the medium; transmission describes pushing all packet bits onto a link. A finite queue drops packets when full. Queueing grows rapidly as long-run arrival rate approaches service rate.

CORE

Packet switching and circuit switching

Packet switching statistically multiplexes users: each packet uses a link when scheduled, making it efficient for bursty traffic but allowing delay and loss. Circuit switching reserves resources for a session, providing predictable capacity but wasting idle reservations. Store-and-forward means a router normally receives the complete packet before transmitting it on the next link.

LAYERS

Encapsulation and scope

  1. Application: network applications and messages.
  2. Transport: process-to-process segments.
  3. Network: host-to-host datagrams and routing.
  4. Link: one-hop frames.
  5. Physical: bits and signals.

Each sender layer adds a header; the receiver removes headers in reverse order. Layering separates concerns but can duplicate functions or hide useful information.

SECURITY

Assume an adversarial network

Attackers may eavesdrop, modify traffic, impersonate endpoints, install malware, or exhaust resources. Confidentiality, integrity, authentication, authorization, availability, and accountability address different risks. Security must be designed into protocols and operations rather than added after deployment.

PHYSICAL MEDIA

Signals need a medium

Twisted pair is inexpensive and widely deployed. Coaxial cable is shielded and supports shared broadband systems. Fiber carries light with high rate, long reach, and resistance to electromagnetic interference. Radio supports mobility and rapid deployment but experiences path loss, interference, fading, and regulation. Propagation speed is typically a large fraction of the speed of light, while transmission rate depends on signaling, bandwidth, and noise.

NETWORK OF NETWORKS

How global reachability scales

End systems connect to access ISPs. Access and regional ISPs connect through transit providers, peering links, and Internet exchange points. Large content providers operate private backbones and edge sites near users. Customer-provider relationships typically involve payment; settlement-free peering exchanges traffic when both parties see mutual value. No single organization controls the whole Internet.

DELAY WORKED TRACE

Calculate one packet across multiple links

For a packet of L bits crossing N store-and-forward links with rates Rᵢ, total transmission component is Σ L/Rᵢ. Add propagation Σ dᵢ/sᵢ, router processing, and queueing at every hop. With equal-rate links and no queueing, the first packet needs approximately N·L/R plus propagation. A long file can be pipelined: later packets occupy different links simultaneously, so steady-state throughput approaches the bottleneck rate rather than one packet per end-to-end delay.

Traceroute reveals hop-by-hop round-trip samples by sending probes whose TTL values expire at successive routers. It exposes a forward-path approximation and timing, but return paths may differ and routers may deprioritize responses.

PROTOCOL EXAMPLE

Four parts of a protocol

Consider a browser request: syntax defines the HTTP request line and headers; semantics define GET, Host, and status codes; ordering requires request before response; actions specify that the server returns data or an error and that the client may retry or redirect. Naming only a message format is incomplete—the state machine and error behavior are part of the protocol.

HISTORY

Why today’s design looks this way

Early packet-switching research established statistical multiplexing and store-and-forward networks. Internetworking joined heterogeneous networks through a common packet layer. TCP/IP deployment, DNS, and the NSFNET era expanded academic networking. The Web and commercial ISPs drove rapid public adoption. Broadband, Wi-Fi, mobile networks, cloud data centers, CDNs, software control, and hyperscale private backbones later reshaped where traffic originates and how close services are placed to users.

1.7 Concept check

Routing versus forwarding?

Routing computes the path through the network. Forwarding moves one packet from an input interface to the correct output interface.

Why does queueing delay vary?

It depends on how many packets arrived earlier and the current arrival rate relative to link capacity.

When does packet loss occur?

Most commonly when a finite queue is full; packets can also be discarded because of detected corruption, policy, an invalid header, or an unavailable route.

What does encapsulation achieve?

Each layer adds control information needed to deliver its own service while preserving the upper-layer payload.
